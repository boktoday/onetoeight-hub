A briefing for parents of preteens & teens
2:07 AM

The house is quiet. Your mind isn't.

Somewhere down the hall, a phone glows under a doona. And you're awake running the same loop — who are they talking to, what are they seeing, are they okay, will they be okay? You're not overthinking it. The data says these worries are rational. This page names them, backs them with Australian evidence, and shows you a way through — for your child, your family, and the community they'll grow into.

Part one

Eight things that keep you awake

Not vague dread — specific, nameable fears. Every one of them shows up in Australian research on parents of 8–17 year olds. Naming them is the first step, because a named worry can be acted on and a nameless one just circles.

01 · CONTACT

Who are they talking to?

Strangers, older kids, people with agendas. Grooming and sexual extortion are real, and they happen in the apps you can't see into.

0% of parents don't feel fully aware of their child's online activities
02 · CONTENT

What are they seeing?

Violence, pornography, self-harm, hate — often stumbled into by accident, not searched for. Once seen, it can't be unseen.

0% of parents say they struggle to keep their child safe online
03 · TIME

Where do the hours go?

The scroll that eats sleep, homework, and the dinner-table conversation. It's not weak willpower — the apps are engineered to win.

0% hope their child can "look after themselves" online — hope isn't a plan
04 · MOOD

Are they okay, really?

The comparison spiral, the group chat that goes quiet, the body-image feed. Anxiety and low mood track closely with heavy social use.

0% of parents believe they talk openly — but kids report telling less
05 · SAFETY SKILLS

Do they know what to do when it goes wrong?

Sextortion, live location-sharing, online dating — most parents have never even raised these, let alone prepared their child for them.

0% of parents have discussed sexual extortion with their child
06 · THE FUTURE

What is this doing to who they're becoming?

Attention, patience, real-world skills, the ability to sit with boredom and build something. The worry isn't just today — it's the adult they'll be.

0% of parents have discussed how generative AI can be used or misused
07 · THE BAN

Did the social media ban actually change anything?

Australia banned under-16 accounts in December 2025. But most kids are still on the platforms — and the law never taught them what to do with the time.

0% of kids kept their Instagram, Snapchat & TikTok accounts anyway
08 · ALONE IN IT

Am I the only one fighting this?

Every other parent looks like they've got it handled. Nobody compares notes. So you carry it alone — and your kid's friends' parents do too.

0% of parents don't use any parental controls at all

Part three

Parents are doing the guarding. Nobody's doing the building.

The research shows Australian parents are far from passive. The gap isn't effort — it's direction. Protection removes risk; it doesn't install capability.

🛡️ What parents are already doing

  • Setting rules about being online 92%
  • Talking openly about online safety 93%
  • Encouraging kids to speak up when something goes wrong 84%
  • Actively monitoring their child's online activity 68%
  • Using parental controls (most, though not all) 71%

🕳️ What's falling through the cracks

  • Sexual extortion — the fastest-growing threat, barely discussed 8%
  • Generative AI — how it can be used and misused 11%
  • Algorithmic influence — how feeds shape what kids think 26%
  • Awareness of emerging tech — VR, haptics, live location-sharing low
  • Anything to replace the scroll — the ban freed up time, but nothing filled it —

Part five

What changes — for the three circles that matter

A formation program isn't a product your child consumes. It's a practice your family and community grow into. Here's the honest picture of what eight weeks tends to shift.

🌱

For the young person

  • A daily rhythm that isn't organised around a feed
  • Named skills for the online risks they'll actually meet
  • Confidence that comes from competence, not likes
  • Eight weeks of small, kept promises to themselves
  • A language for talking to adults about hard things
🏠

For the family

  • Conversations that start from a shared practice, not a confrontation
  • A reason to put phones down that isn't a punishment
  • Less policing, more mentoring — the relationship improves
  • Parents who finally have a script for the hard topics
  • The 2am loop gets quieter, because there's a plan
🤝

For the community

  • A pod of eight families who know and look out for each other
  • Young people who show up — to each other, and locally
  • Shared norms about screens, so no single family stands alone
  • A generation that builds things, not just scrolls past them
  • Adults and teens in the same room, on the same page
Part six · how we reach parents

The plan for getting this into parents' hands

Parents of preteens and teens aren't on TikTok looking for a formation program. They're in school newsletters, church rosters, sports clubs, Facebook groups, and each other's kitchens. The plan meets them there — with empathy first, never a sales pitch.

Phase 1 · Weeks 1–4

Lead with the worry, not the product

Publish and share the "2:07 AM" piece (this page) as a free, honest resource. It names the problem with real Australian data and only then mentions onetoeight. Parents share things that make them feel understood — this is the shareable object. Pair it with a one-page "what the ban actually did" explainer, since that's the question every parent is asking right now.

Phase 2 · Weeks 3–8

Go through the trusted gatekeepers

Parents trust institutions more than ads. Pitch the program to the adults who already have the room: school chaplains and wellbeing coordinators, youth pastors, sports club coordinators, and parent Facebook group admins. Offer a free 20-minute talk at a parent night — "Raising kids in a screen-born world" — with the program as the takeaway, not the pitch.

Phase 3 · Weeks 6–12

Make the first pod effortless

The barrier isn't interest, it's logistics. Offer a "starter pod" kit: a ready-made 8-week plan, a host guide for one volunteer parent, and printable materials. One parent recruits seven friends from school pickup or church — that's the whole funnel. The pod is the product and the marketing at once.

Phase 4 · Ongoing

Let parents tell parents

Collect short, honest stories from parents after each pod — "what changed at our dinner table" — and turn them into the next shareable. Word of mouth between parents is the highest-converting channel there is; the job is just to give it something true to carry.

A note on honesty: The figures above are drawn from the two eSafety reports cited, which are the most current national Australian data on this topic.
